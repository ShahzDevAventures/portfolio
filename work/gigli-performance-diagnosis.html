Gigli Performance:When field and lab disagree

Gigli, a Shopify Plus brand, had a mobile field CLS of 0.9 in PageSpeed while every lab run was green. The performance theme we had just published was blamed and reverted, and the field number did not move. So I stopped trusting the lab and instrumented the field.

01Evidence trail

The field said 0.9. The lab said 0.009.

PageSpeed's field data for gigli.com on mobile showed a CLS of 0.9, and it had been 0.0 on 20 July. Every lab run was green: Lighthouse put the homepage at 0.009 and the product page at 0.051. We had just published a performance theme, so the publish was blamed and reverted. The field number stayed where it was.

The performance theme was a real improvement on the lab side. Measured on the preview against the live theme, median of three Lighthouse runs on mobile: LCP 11.65 s to 6.96 s, total bytes 6,323 KB to 3,296 KB, requests 466 to 285, lab CLS 0.000. None of that explained a field CLS of 0.9, and a revert that did not move the number was itself evidence the theme was never the cause.

It was not the first time the easy reading had been wrong on this store. The original audit flagged a 2,289 KB hero PNG as the biggest problem. That figure came from curl's default Accept header; a real browser asks for WebP, and Shopify's CDN was already serving the same image at 107 KB, an 18.5 times difference. I wrote the correction into the audit and moved the hero down the list, behind 2,843 KB of third-party JavaScript across 187 requests. The lesson carried over: measure what the visitor's browser does, not what a tool reports.

02Problem statement
Lab tools load a page once and never touch it, so they cannot see shifts from a looping animation or from an app popup a visitor has to dismiss. When the field disagrees with the lab, re-running the lab and reverting the theme both answer the wrong question. The diagnosis needed a measurement taken the way a visitor's browser takes it.

04How we arrived at the solution

Two themes, one observer, then the profiler.

I ran the observer on both themes at a mobile viewport. The new theme produced zero layout shifts through a full load and a full-page scroll. The live theme produced a continuous shift loop: 121 events in eight seconds from an "as seen on" logo marquee animating layout properties. That section had been rewritten on the new theme, which is why the new theme was clean. The revert had reinstated a CLS bug.

The marquee was not the whole 0.9. The rest sat in a stack of app-injected popup iframes: a full-screen scratch-card, an offer tab, a chat bar, all new inside the window where the field number regressed. Chrome's field CLS counts shifts inside iframes weighted by how much of the viewport they cover, and the scratch-card covers roughly the whole screen on mobile. Lab never interacts with it, so lab stays green. It is app-injected, so it survived the revert, which is why the field number survived the revert too.

In the same pass I read the four Liquid profiler captures the developer had taken. Homepage server CPU was 107 ms on the live theme and 159 ms on the new one; the product page went from 140 ms to 389 ms, up 178%, with the caveat that these were single captures and possibly different products. Two causes stood out. An 83 KB icon snippet was re-evaluated on every render at about 5.5 ms a call. And a for product in collections.all.products loop was costing 11 ms against 1.7 ms on the live theme. It existed on both themes, on every page, and in no repo file: it lived in editor-configured content, rendered quick-buy markup per product, and grew with every product added to the catalogue. Code review could never have seen it.

The plan I handed over, in order: paired profiler captures on the same product URL and homepage on both themes, three runs each, to attribute the regression precisely. Split the hot icons into one small snippet each, which pays on every page on both themes. Find the catalogue loop in the editor content and replace it with a capped source. Pause the scratch-card campaign and watch the daily field trend. Then re-publish with nothing else on the train.

05What happened

The revert had fixed nothing and broken one thing.

The theme that was rolled back was the one with zero shifts. The theme that stayed live had the marquee loop, the app popups, and a catalogue-wide Liquid loop nobody had put in the repo. Everything on the plan went to the developer with the capture method written down, so the next publish gets judged on measurements rather than a story.
