01Evidence trail

The first plan was one website that behaved like two.

Gigli makes hemp-derived THC drinks and sells them through thousands of retailers. A THC ban was on the horizon, so they built a zero-THC functional line to keep the company going, and their media agency, DOE Media, brought us in to launch it on Shopify.

The first plan was one website that behaved like two. A visitor answered the 21+ question and the site changed around them, THC or functional, driven by GrowthBook flags and metaobjects. It was an elegant solution, but after speaking with the ads team we learned it did not serve their needs: ad platforms crawl the entire domain, and any THC product in the user journey makes the website ineligible to run ads on Google, Meta and TikTok Shop. So the functional line needed its own website with nothing THC on it.

In the first week of September we split the business in two. The THC store moved to giglinfused.com behind 21+ gating, and gigli.com became a brand-new store for the functional line with its own Klaviyo, pixels and ad accounts, and no link between the sites. That meant four parties had to agree inside a week, the merchant, DOE, their ads advisor and Sirge, and they did.

02Problem statement
Move a live business onto two websites that never touch, without losing a subscriber, a redirect or an ad account, and build the second one from scratch in weeks.
03How we arrived at the solution

Everything attached to the old store had to land in the right place.

Splitting a live business meant everything attached to the old store had to land in the right place, so I audited it: fourteen tracking pixels, ten app embeds, scripts pasted into the theme, each given an owner and a date. Old URLs would die on the new domain, so 261 redirects were written and tested before the switch. Two stores shared one warehouse, so ShipStation became a single account feeding both.

But the new store did not exist yet. It needed 29 sections built from a Figma file in weeks, so I built them with a pipeline that measured every section against the design before a developer saw it, which is why the store matched the mockups and launched without visual bugs. The merchant plans to move to Plus, so we built on a standard plan with the upgrade designed in: checkout, bundles and subscriptions carry over.

The old site was also slow, 6 MB and 466 requests on a phone and 11.6 seconds before the hero appeared, so I cut 4.9 MB of dead assets and split a vendor bundle shipped without source. The new store shows its hero in one second.

04What happened

The split did what it was for.

We cut over on the evening of 19 October. gigli.com became an alias of the THC store while the primary moved to giglinfused.com, with a popup telling customers where the THC range had gone. Every page and all 261 redirects came back green within the hour, but one DNS record had not been updated, so Recharge portal links broke for a day until it was. The rollback was never used.

Meta, Google and TikTok approved ad accounts for gigli.com, and TikTok Shop approved the store.

05What they have now

A marketing manager can change the website.

Before the split, every change to Gigli's website went through a web agency. A store built this fast only pays off if the people who own it can change it, so the handoff was not a folder of files. Gigli's marketing lead, with no technical background, got a Claude agent in their Slack that knows the design system, the build rules and the brand assets. They ask it for a new section, a change to an existing one, or a landing page, and it builds it.

Our developer checks each change in GitHub before it goes live, and for the first month updates the agent's rules and QA guidelines as it learns how the team works. The Loom walkthroughs cover everything else. Their team runs the store now, and no agency is in the loop for day-to-day changes.
